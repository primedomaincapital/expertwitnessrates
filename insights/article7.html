How Long Does It Take to Prepare an Expert Witness for Trial?

Trial was eleven weeks out and the records were not complete. The four-to-six-week clock for a damages opinion starts when the file is complete, not when the expert is hired.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

The call came on a Monday. Trial was set for eleven weeks out, the damages expert had just been retained, and the partner wanted to know one thing: could the expert be ready? The answer depended on a question nobody had asked yet — how complete were the records? The medical file was missing two years of treatment notes. The financials were unaudited. The damages model existed only as a spreadsheet the client had built himself. The expert's honest answer was four to six weeks from the day the records were complete, and the records were not complete. The trial date was not the deadline. The records were.

The clock that actually controls the calendar

The first thing to understand about expert preparation is that the court sets one deadline and the work sets another. Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 26(a)(2)(D) requires initial expert disclosures at least 90 days before trial, with rebuttal disclosures due within 30 days of the opposing party's disclosure. Miss the 90-day deadline and Rule 37(c)(1) bars the expert from testifying at all, unless the failure was substantially justified or harmless. That is the hard edge. Everything else is a negotiation between the expert's schedule and the state of the file.

The work itself has a floor. A straightforward damages opinion — a clean set of records, a defined question, a single methodology — takes four to six weeks from the day the records are complete, according to Joey Friedman, a CPA who has built his practice on exactly that kind of engagement. That is the best case. A medical causation opinion, where the expert must review thousands of pages of treatment notes, imaging, and literature, runs longer. A case with incomplete records does not run at all until the gaps are closed, and closing them is usually the retaining lawyer's job, not the expert's.

What preparation actually consists of

The written report is the center of the work, and Rule 26(a)(2)(B) prescribes its contents in detail: a complete statement of every opinion the witness will express and the basis and reasons for each, the facts and data considered, the exhibits that will summarize or support the opinions, the witness's qualifications including a list of all publications authored in the previous ten years, and a list of all other cases in which the witness testified at trial or by deposition in the previous four years. A report that omits any of those elements is not merely incomplete. It is a disclosure failure, and the sanction is exclusion.

Around the report sits the rest of the preparation: the document review, the methodology statement, the deposition, and the trial testimony itself. The deposition is where most experts are made or broken, and it is usually scheduled weeks after the report is served — which means the expert's preparation has two peaks, not one. The first is the report. The second is the deposition, where opposing counsel tests every sentence of the report against the underlying records. The third is trial, where the expert must explain the same opinions to twelve people who have never read the report. Each peak requires its own preparation, and the gaps between them are where experts lose their command of the file.

The Rule 702 layer

Since the December 1, 2023 amendment to Federal Rule of Evidence 702, preparation includes a step that did not exist in the same form before: proving admissibility. The proponent must now show by a preponderance of the evidence that the expert's opinion rests on sufficient facts, reliable principles and methods, and a reliable application of those methods to the facts of the case. A distinguished CV no longer carries an unreliable opinion. That means the expert's preparation must include a methodology statement that can survive a Daubert challenge — not just a conclusion, but a documented chain from data to method to opinion, with the literature cited and the alternatives addressed.

The exclusion data explains why this step cannot be rushed. Across more than 1,500 Daubert challenges to financial experts over 14 years, 46 percent succeeded, according to PwC's long-running study. Roughly a third of challenged experts are at least partially excluded. The pattern holds across specialties: reliability is the leading cause of exclusion, and the experts most often excluded are the ones whose opinions outrun their methods. An expert prepared in three weeks with a thin methodology is not a bargain. They are a Daubert motion waiting to happen.

The cost of unreadiness

When an expert is not ready, the consequences are not measured in weeks. They are measured in exclusion. Rule 37(c)(1) bars the undisclosed or unprepared expert from testifying, and the sanction falls on the party, not the expert. The replacement expert, retained late, charges a premium for the compressed timeline and inherits a file they did not build — and the report they produce in three weeks is the report most likely to fail a Daubert challenge. The 46 percent success rate for Daubert challenges against financial experts, from PwC's study, is not evenly distributed: it concentrates in the cases where the expert was retained late, prepared quickly, and disclosed at the deadline. The calendar is not the enemy of preparation. The late start is.

The counterargument: faster is sometimes better

None of this means the longest preparation is the best preparation. An expert who takes six months to produce a report has often taken six months because the retaining lawyer sent the file in pieces, or because the expert is juggling twenty cases, or because the opinion kept changing as the facts developed. Delay is not diligence. The best-prepared experts are usually the ones retained earliest, with a complete file and a clear question — and the clearest sign of a well-run engagement is that the expert's first draft arrives before the deadline, not the night before it.

There is also a real cost to over-preparation. Every additional week of review is another week of billing at $450 to $600 an hour, and a report that runs to two hundred pages is not twice as persuasive as a report that runs to forty. The experts who command the highest rates are often the ones who know what to leave out. Preparation is not measured in hours. It is measured in whether the opinion survives contact with the other side.

What this means for retaining counsel

Hire early, and send the complete file at once. The four-to-six-week clock for a straightforward opinion starts when the records are complete, not when the engagement letter is signed — and every missing document restarts it. Ask for a preparation schedule in writing: records review, report draft, deposition prep, trial prep, with dates attached to each. And build the schedule backwards from the 90-day disclosure deadline, not forwards from the retention date, because the disclosure deadline is the one the court enforces.

Second, budget for the methodology, not just the hours. The expert who spends a week documenting why their method is reliable is the expert who survives the Daubert hearing. The one who spends that week on additional record review is the one who gets excluded on a method they never defended. The cheapest preparation is the kind that ends with the testimony admitted. Everything else is expensive, however fast it was.
