What Do Expert Witnesses Charge Per Hour by Specialty?

Three quotes for the same damages expert: $450, $650, and $950 an hour. In 2026, an expert's rate is not a measure of quality. It is a measure of scarcity — and of risk.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 20, 2026 · 6 min read

The email contained three quotes for the same job: a damages expert for a commercial dispute, forty hours of review, a written report, one day of deposition. The first quote was $450 an hour. The second was $650. The third was $950 — for the same specialty, the same scope, the same court. The partner staring at the three numbers had no way to know which one was the market rate, which one was a bargain, and which one was a trap. That confusion is the normal state of the expert witness market. There is no published rate card, no exchange, no ticker. There are only surveys, and the surveys disagree with each other by just enough to make every negotiation a guess.

The average, and why it moved

The most cited number in the market comes from ExpertPages, whose 2026 fee data puts the average expert witness rate at roughly $465 an hour — up from $451 in 2024, the previous record. That 3 percent rise looks modest until it is set against the longer trend: SEAK's national fee survey of nearly 1,600 experts puts the medians at $450 an hour for review, $475 for deposition, and $500 for trial testimony. The Expert Institute's fee data lands in the same range, with medical experts near $500 an hour for review and about $600 for testimony. Three independent surveys, three different methodologies, and they converge on the same message: the middle of the market now sits between $450 and $500 an hour, and it is rising.

The averages hide the spread that actually determines a budget. Medical experts average about $597 an hour for testimony — the highest of any category — with orthopedic surgeons and neurosurgeons commanding $600 to $1,000 an hour. Economics and financial damages experts average about $525. Engineering experts typically bill $450 to $700 an hour and above, depending on the subspecialty and whether the work is review or testimony. The gap between the cheapest and the most expensive expert in the same case can run threefold, and it is driven less by quality than by scarcity: board-certified, active-practice specialists in narrow fields are rare, and rare is expensive.

Review, deposition, trial: three different prices

The rate card is not one number but three, and the difference between them is where budgets break. Review work — reading records, forming opinions, drafting the report — is the cheapest tier, and it is where most of the hours live. Deposition testimony typically runs 10 to 30 percent above review rates. Trial testimony runs higher still, and it comes with half-day or full-day minimums: an expert who testifies for ninety minutes still bills for the day. Full case involvement — review, report, deposition, trial — runs $40,000 to $50,000 or more per expert in a records-heavy case, plus $2,500 to $4,000 a day in travel-and-testify day rates. A medical malpractice case that settles after expert workup typically consumes $30,000 to $75,000 in hard costs; one that goes through trial commonly runs $75,000 to $150,000 or more, before anyone counts attorney time.

The structure matters more than the headline rate. An expert who bills $600 an hour and finishes the review in twenty hours costs less than an expert who bills $450 and takes forty. The market has no mechanism to reward the faster expert — hourly billing pays for time, not for the opinion — which is why the retaining lawyer's real job is not finding the cheapest rate. It is finding the expert whose hours are honest.

Who pays is as important as what they pay. The market's roughly $2 billion in annual spend flows from three directions: plaintiff firms hiring damages and medical experts, defense firms and insurers hiring their own, and corporations hiring experts for internal investigations and regulatory matters. This site's own benchmark puts total U.S. expert witness spend above $5 billion a year — the difference between the two figures is the gap between testimony work and the broader consulting market that surrounds it. Either way, the buyer is almost always a law firm or an insurer, and the rate is almost always negotiated by someone who has hired experts before. The first-time buyer is the exception, and the market prices their inexperience.

What the rate card does not show

Two charges sit outside the hourly rate and surprise first-time buyers every time. The first is the retainer: most physicians request $2,000 to $3,000 upfront before taking a case, and retainers across specialties typically run $2,000 to $10,000 depending on the expert and the scope. The second is the cancellation schedule, which is where the fine print lives. A trial date that moves a week before the hearing can trigger a full day rate, and a deposition cancelled inside 48 hours can cost the full fee. The rate card quotes the hour. The engagement letter prices the risk.

The market itself is roughly a $2 billion industry, and it is growing because the cases that need experts are getting more complex, not fewer. When Sermo asked physicians about expert witness work, 15 percent said they currently do it, 23 percent have in the past, and 46 percent said they have not but would consider it. Twenty-eight percent pointed to the opportunity to earn additional income. The supply side is responding to the money. The demand side is responding to the amended Rule 702, which since December 2023 has required the proponent to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that an expert's opinion rests on sufficient facts, reliable methods, and a reliable application of those methods. The rule raised the price of a bad expert. It did not lower the price of a good one.

The counterargument: the rate is the wrong number to negotiate

None of this means the hourly rate is the right thing to bargain over. A $1,000-an-hour surgeon with a thin methodology is a more expensive exclusion than a $450-an-hour generalist with a bulletproof one — and the exclusion costs the case, not just the fee. Across more than 1,500 Daubert challenges to financial experts over 14 years, 46 percent succeeded, according to PwC's long-running study, and roughly a third of challenged experts are at least partially excluded. The experts most often excluded are the ones whose opinions outrun their methods, and no discount on their rate changes that.

The honest read is that rates now encode two premiums at once: a scarcity premium for credentials that are genuinely rare, and an admissibility premium for experts whose methods have survived challenge. The first is visible in the rate card. The second is not — it shows up in the expert's prior admissions, their published methodology, and whether they have ever been excluded. A lawyer who negotiates only the first premium is buying the visible half of the price.

What this means for retaining counsel

Budget by task, not by hour. Ask for the retainer, the review rate, the deposition rate, the trial day rate, the cancellation schedule, and the travel policy in writing before the engagement starts — and compare the total across experts, not the headline number. A $650-an-hour expert with a $2,000 retainer and a 50 percent cancellation fee can cost less than a $450-an-hour expert with a $10,000 retainer and a 100 percent cancellation fee, depending on how the case moves.

Second, vet for method before you vet for credentials, and pay for the vetting. An expert's exclusion history, their prior admissions under the amended Rule 702, and a written statement of methodology are worth more than a hundred dollars an hour of rate difference. The $465 average is not the price of expertise. It is the price of entry. The real price is what happens when the testimony does not survive — and no rate card lists that number.
