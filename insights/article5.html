Rule 702’s New Math: Why the Cheapest Expert Is Now the Most Expensive

The deposition lasted four hours, the expert billed $1,000 an hour, and the testimony never reached the jury. In 2026, an expert’s rate is not a measure of quality. It is a measure of risk.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 9, 2026 · 5 min read

The deposition lasted four hours, the expert billed $1,000 an hour, and the testimony never reached the jury. The judge excluded it — not because the expert was unqualified, but because the methodology did not hold up under the amended Rule 702. The retaining lawyer had bought the most expensive name on the list and received, in return, the most expensive lesson in the case: in 2026, an expert's rate is not a measure of quality. It is a measure of risk. And the cheapest expert on the list is often the most expensive one you can hire.

What experts actually charge

The market has never been pricier. Average expert-witness hourly fees reached a record of roughly $451 an hour in the most recent fee surveys, according to the 2026 State of Expert Witness Testimony report, which synthesizes the ExpertPages and SEAK 2024 fee surveys. Medical experts average about $597 an hour — the highest of any category — with orthopedic surgeons and neurosurgeons commanding $600 to $1,000 an hour. Economics and financial damages experts average about $525. SEAK's national fee survey of nearly 1,600 experts puts the medians at $450 an hour for review, $475 for deposition, and $500 for trial testimony. The Expert Institute's fee data puts medical experts near $500 an hour for review and about $600 for testimony, with surgical specialties commonly $600 to $1,000 and above. Deposition and trial testimony typically run 10 to 30 percent above review rates, with half-day or full-day minimums common for trial, and most physicians request an upfront retainer of $2,000 to $3,000 before taking a case.

The totals are what break budgets. Full case involvement — review, report, deposition, trial — runs $40,000 to $50,000 or more per expert in a records-heavy case, plus $2,500 to $4,000 a day in travel-and-testify day rates. A medical malpractice case that settles after expert workup typically consumes $30,000 to $75,000 in hard costs; one that goes through trial commonly runs $75,000 to $150,000 or more, before anyone counts attorney time. With two or three plaintiff experts plus rebuttal work, expert fees alone routinely account for half or more of total case costs. The market for expertise is roughly a $2 billion industry, and it is growing because the cases that need experts are getting more complex, not fewer.

The rule that repriced the market

The reason rates now function as a risk signal is the December 1, 2023 amendment to Federal Rule of Evidence 702 — the most significant change to the expert-testimony standard in nearly 25 years. Under the amended rule, the proponent must prove by a preponderance of the evidence that the expert's opinion rests on sufficient facts, reliable principles and methods, and a reliable application of those methods to the facts of the case. A distinguished CV no longer carries an unreliable opinion. Courts now scrutinize the reliable application of methodology, and the exclusions follow the method, not the resume: differential diagnoses that do not reliably rule out alternatives, causation opinions unsupported by literature, and opinions stretching beyond the expert's actual clinical practice.

The exclusion data is stark. Across more than 1,500 Daubert challenges to financial experts over 14 years, 46 percent succeeded, according to PwC's long-running study. Roughly a third of challenged experts are at least partially excluded. Exclusion rates differ sharply by discipline: appraisers about 38 percent, accountants about 32 percent, economists about 27 percent. The pattern holds across specialties — reliability is the leading cause of exclusion, and the experts most often excluded are the ones whose opinions outrun their methods. Meanwhile the supply side is responding to the money: when Sermo asked physicians about expert witness work, 15 percent said they currently do it, 23 percent have in the past, and 46 percent said they have not but would consider it. Twenty-eight percent pointed to the opportunity to earn additional income.

The counterargument: price is a poor proxy for admissibility

None of this means the expensive expert is the safe one. A $1,000-an-hour surgeon with a thin methodology is a more expensive exclusion than a $450-an-hour generalist with a bulletproof one — and the exclusion costs the case, not just the fee. The premium end of the market is also where the "hired gun" problem lives: experts who testify frequently, for one side, at rates that invite the other side to argue bias. A court that sees an expert earning a substantial share of income from testimony will ask harder questions about independence, and a high rate can become evidence against the expert rather than for them. The market's highest rates reward scarcity — board-certified, active-practice surgeons in narrow specialties — but scarcity and reliability are different things, and only one of them survives a Daubert hearing.

The honest read is that rates now encode two different premiums at once: a scarcity premium for credentials that are genuinely rare, and an admissibility premium for experts whose methods have survived challenge. The first is visible in the rate card. The second is not — it shows up in the expert's prior admissions, their published methodology, and whether they have ever been excluded. The buyers who pay only for the first premium are the ones who learn about the second in court.

What this means for retaining counsel

Vet for method before you vet for credentials. Ask for the expert's exclusion history, their prior admissions under the amended rule, and a written statement of methodology before the retainer is signed — not after the report is written. Budget for exclusion risk, not just hours: a second expert, retained early, costs far less than a replacement expert retained after a Daubert loss. And hire early, because the experts whose methods survive challenge are the ones with the longest lead times and the highest rates — and the gap between their rate and the excluded expert's rate is the cheapest insurance in the case. The $451 average is not the price of expertise. It is the price of entry. The real price is what happens when the testimony does not survive.
