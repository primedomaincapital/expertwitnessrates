In 2023, a forensic accountant named Greg Regan billed 340 hours on a single commercial litigation matter. At $950 an hour, that’s $323,000 — for one case, for testimony about numbers. Regan is not unusual. He’s mid-tier. The top economic experts in antitrust and securities cases routinely bill $1,200 to $1,500 per hour. Medical experts in high-stakes malpractice trials can command $1,000 per hour for testimony, with multi-day retainers paid upfront. A prominent neurosurgeon in California reportedly charges $2,500 per hour for trial testimony, with a $25,000 minimum.

Welcome to the expert witness economy — a roughly $2 billion annual industry that operates almost entirely out of public view, governed by few regulations, driven by extraordinary hourly rates, and absolutely critical to how American justice functions. Every year, tens of thousands of experts testify in civil and criminal cases. Their fees are paid by litigants, reimbursed by insurers, and ultimately baked into the cost of the legal system. Yet almost no one outside the litigation world understands how this market works.

How the market is structured

The expert witness industry has three layers. At the top are the marquee names — the Nobel laureates, the former FDA commissioners, the celebrity forensic specialists — who can command virtually any fee because their credentials end the debate before it starts. When a former Surgeon General testifies about public health, the jury isn’t evaluating methodology. They’re starstruck. These experts might work 50 hours a year as witnesses and earn more than most law firm partners.

The middle layer is where the real volume sits. These are the practicing professionals — engineers, doctors, accountants, economists — who dedicate 10 to 30 percent of their time to expert work. A structural engineer who investigates building failures. An orthopedic surgeon who reviews malpractice claims. A valuation expert who opines on business disputes. They charge $400 to $800 per hour, work steadily, and often earn more from expert work than from their day jobs. For many, it’s the most lucrative thing they do.

The bottom layer is the high-volume, low-margin world of independent medical examinations, vocational assessments, and routine testing. These experts might charge $250 to $400 per hour but handle hundreds of cases per year. It’s a grind — but a profitable one for those who systematize it.

Connecting these layers is a referral ecosystem of expert witness directories, placement firms, and what the industry euphemistically calls “consulting companies.” Firms like TASA, Round Table Group, and GLG (which started in expert networks for investors and expanded into litigation) maintain databases of thousands of experts and take a cut of every engagement. The largest operate essentially as talent agencies for testimony.

The pricing puzzle

Expert witness pricing follows no standard schedule, which is precisely why it’s so lucrative — and so controversial. In theory, experts charge their “normal professional rate.” In practice, the rate for litigation work often bears no resemblance to what the same professional charges in their regular practice.

Consider medicine. A cardiologist might earn $500,000 per year in clinical practice — roughly $250 per hour. As an expert witness, that same cardiologist charges $600 to $800 per hour for record review and $1,000 or more for deposition and trial testimony. The premium reflects not just the time but the unpleasantness: cross-examination, schedule disruption, and the professional risk of being labeled a “hired gun.”

Economics experts show the widest spreads. Academic economists at top universities might charge $800 to $1,200 per hour for litigation consulting — several times their imputed academic hourly rate. The justification is opportunity cost and the adversarial nature of the work. The reality is that antitrust and securities cases involve billions of dollars, and the expert’s fee is rounding error. When the stakes are $5 billion, nobody negotiates over whether the economist charges $900 or $1,100.

This creates a peculiar market dynamic. In most professional services, higher prices reduce demand. In expert witnessing, higher prices can increase demand — because the fee itself signals credibility. A $300-per-hour expert looks cheap. A $900-per-hour expert looks authoritative. Attorneys selecting experts aren’t just buying analysis. They’re buying the jury’s perception of authority, and price is part of the packaging.

The hired-gun problem

The expert witness industry’s original sin is obvious: experts are paid by the side that hires them. This doesn’t automatically make their testimony biased — many experts are scrupulously honest, and their professional reputations depend on it. But the structural incentive is undeniable, and it’s the first thing every cross-examining attorney attacks.

“Doctor, you’re being paid $750 an hour to be here today, correct?” This question, or some variant, is asked in virtually every trial involving expert testimony. The follow-up is always the same: “And how much have you earned from [hiring party] in total?” The goal isn’t to prove the expert is lying. It’s to suggest that $150,000 in fees might unconsciously color even an honest professional’s judgment.

The industry has developed defenses. Reputable experts maintain a balance of plaintiff and defense work — typically aiming for no more than 60% from either side. They publish their fee schedules openly. They decline cases where the science doesn’t support the hiring party’s position. The best experts will tell you about the times they told a hiring attorney, “Your case is weak, and I won’t help you pretend otherwise.” Those stories are their marketing.

But the bottom of the market is murkier. There are experts — everyone in the industry knows who they are — who testify exclusively for one side, always reach the same conclusion, and earn seven figures annually doing it. They’re rarely excluded under Daubert because their methodology is technically defensible. They’re just professionally committed to a viewpoint. The legal system tolerates them because the adversarial process is supposed to expose bias through cross-examination. Whether it actually does is one of the system’s open questions.

Technology is reshaping the expert search

Finding the right expert used to depend on who you knew. Senior partners had rolodexes of trusted experts built over decades. Young lawyers were at a disadvantage. That information asymmetry supported premium pricing — if you didn’t know alternatives existed, you paid whatever the known expert charged.

The internet flattened this. Expert directories, AI-powered matching services, and platforms that algorithmically pair cases with experts have commoditized the search process. A solo practitioner in Boise can now find a qualified structural engineer as efficiently as a white-shoe firm. This should, in theory, compress fees through competition.

It hasn’t — yet. The reason is that expertise, unlike most services, resists commoditization at the top end. An AI can find you fifty qualified cardiologists. It can’t tell you which one will be compelling on the witness stand, handle cross-examination gracefully, and explain complex medicine in language a jury understands. The trial performance premium — the difference between a good expert and a great witness — remains stubbornly human and stubbornly expensive.

Where technology is having impact is in the middle market: record review, preliminary analysis, and expert-adjacent tasks that don’t require testimony. AI-assisted document review and data analysis are reducing the hours experts bill for preparation, even as their testimony rates hold steady. The total cost per case may be falling even as hourly rates rise.

The regulatory vacuum

Remarkably, expert witnessing is almost entirely unregulated. There are no licensing requirements specific to expert witnesses. No mandatory fee disclosures (though courts can order them). No prohibition on contingency arrangements in most jurisdictions — though the ABA’s Model Rules and most state ethics rules forbid experts from working on contingency, recognizing the obvious corrupting incentive.

This vacuum persists partly because regulation would be genuinely difficult. How do you set fair rates for a market this heterogeneous? What’s the “right” price for a Nobel laureate’s testimony versus a community college engineering instructor’s? Any schedule would be either absurdly complex or absurdly crude.

It persists partly because the current system, for all its flaws, mostly works. The adversarial process — direct examination, cross-examination, Daubert challenges, competing experts — provides a rough quality control. Bad experts get exposed. Good experts get hired again. The market disciplines itself, imperfectly but persistently.

What the economics reveal

Step back, and the expert witness economy looks like a pure market for credibility. The product isn’t really analysis — competent analysis is widely available. The product is persuasive authority: the ability to make a jury believe. That’s why credentials command premiums, why trial experience matters more than publication records, and why the best experts are often the best teachers, not the best researchers.

This has an uncomfortable implication. If what litigants are buying is persuasion rather than truth, then the expert witness system is less a truth-seeking mechanism than a credibility auction. The side with more money hires more credible experts. Justice goes to the highest bidder — not in the crude sense of buying verdicts, but in the subtle sense of buying the most persuasive version of reality.

Defenders of the system argue this critique proves too much. Every part of litigation costs money — better lawyers, more investigation, slicker presentations. Expert witnesses are just one input among many. And the alternative — court-appointed neutral experts — has its own problems, including the difficulty of finding genuinely neutral expertise and the loss of adversarial testing.

The honest assessment is that the expert witness economy is simultaneously essential and distorting. Essential, because modern litigation involves technical questions no lay jury can resolve without help. Distorting, because the help goes disproportionately to whoever can afford the best helpers. The $2 billion industry exists because the justice system needs it. It costs $2 billion because the justice system can’t function without it — and everyone involved knows it.
