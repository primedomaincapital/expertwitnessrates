Retainer vs. Hourly: How Expert Witness Fee Structures Actually Work

The invoice did not match the quote: a forgotten retainer, a full day rate for a trial that moved, a cancellation charge for a deposition that was only rescheduled. The engagement letter had priced it all.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 6 min read

The invoice arrived two weeks after the trial date moved, and it did not match the quote. The expert had billed $450 an hour for review, as agreed. But there was also a $3,400 retainer the partner had forgotten, a full day rate for a trial date that no longer existed, and a cancellation charge for a deposition that had been rescheduled — not cancelled — three days before it was due to happen. The partner's first instinct was to dispute it. The engagement letter's second page said otherwise. The expert had been paid exactly what the letter promised. The partner had simply never read the letter.

Two structures, one engagement

Almost every expert engagement combines the same two elements: a retainer and an hourly rate. The retainer is paid upfront, before the expert opens the file, and it is typically $2,000 to $10,000 depending on the specialty and the scope of the work. Most physicians request $2,000 to $3,000 before taking a case. The retainer is not a deposit on the hourly work. It is a separate charge — a commitment fee that compensates the expert for clearing their calendar, for the opportunity cost of turning away other work, and for the risk that the case settles before the testimony is ever needed. Some retainers are applied against future billings. Many are not. The engagement letter decides, and the letters differ.

The hourly rate covers the work itself, and it is not one rate but three. Review work — reading records, forming opinions, drafting the report — is the cheapest tier. Deposition testimony typically runs 10 to 30 percent above review rates. Trial testimony runs higher still, with half-day or full-day minimums: an expert who testifies for ninety minutes still bills for the day. Full case involvement runs $40,000 to $50,000 or more per expert in a records-heavy case, plus $2,500 to $4,000 a day in travel-and-testify day rates. The rate card quotes the hour. The engagement letter prices the whole case.

The cancellation schedule is where the money hides

The most expensive paragraph in any expert engagement letter is the cancellation policy, and it is the paragraph least often negotiated. Published policies from expert practices show a common tiered structure: cancellation more than fourteen days before the scheduled testimony carries no fee, cancellation seven to thirteen days out costs half the fee, and cancellation inside seven days costs the full fee. A trial date that moves a week before the hearing can trigger a full day rate for a day the expert never works. A deposition rescheduled inside 48 hours can cost the full fee even though the deposition still happens — just on a different day.

The logic is not unreasonable. An expert who blocks three days for a trial turns away other work to do it, and a late cancellation leaves those days empty. The problem is not the policy. It is that the policy is rarely priced into the budget. A case with a $50,000 expert budget and a trial date that moves twice can generate $10,000 in cancellation fees that nobody forecasted, because nobody read the schedule.

Flat fees, day rates, and the structure nobody offers

Some experts offer flat fees for defined pieces of work — a fixed price for the report, a fixed price for the deposition, a day rate for trial. A flat fee for the report transfers the risk of a slow review from the client to the expert, which is why the experts who offer it tend to be the ones who know exactly how long the work takes. A day rate for trial does the same thing in reverse: it caps the client's exposure on the day the expert testifies, but it also means a ninety-minute appearance costs the same as a full day on the stand.

One structure does not exist, and it is the one clients ask for most often: contingency. An expert witness cannot work on a percentage of the recovery. The rule is not a matter of preference. An expert whose pay depends on the outcome is an expert whose independence is compromised, and a court that discovers a contingency arrangement will treat the testimony accordingly. The prohibition is the reason the retainer exists at all — the expert must be paid whether the case wins or loses, because the opinion must be the same either way.

The leverage reverses

The negotiation has a window, and it closes the day the engagement letter is signed. Before retention, the expert wants the case — the work is interesting, the client is a repeat buyer, the referral relationship matters — and the fee terms are flexible. After the report is written, the leverage reverses: the retaining lawyer needs this expert, on this record, and the expert knows it. The cancellation terms, the trial day rate, and the travel policy negotiated before the file is sent are the terms that govern the case. The ones negotiated after the deposition are the ones the expert dictates. Get competing quotes before you retain, not after you need the testimony — and put the quotes in writing, because the expert who quoted $450 an hour in January will not remember the number in September.

The counterargument: the retainer protects the client too

It is easy to read the retainer as a one-sided device, and sometimes it is. But the retainer also protects the retaining lawyer. An expert who has been paid upfront is an expert who has committed to the case — who has cleared the calendar, declined the competing engagement, and invested the time to learn the file. An expert working on a promise is an expert who can walk away when a better-paying matter appears. The retainer is the price of the expert's attention, and attention is the scarcest input in the whole engagement.

The same logic applies to the cancellation fee. A client who can cancel a trial date for free will cancel it casually. A client who pays for the cancellation thinks twice. The fee aligns the incentives: it makes the retaining lawyer treat the expert's calendar as a real constraint, which it is.

What this means for retaining counsel

Get the fee agreement in writing before the file is sent, and read the second page. The agreement should state the retainer and whether it is applied against billings, the review rate, the deposition rate, the trial day rate, the travel policy, and the cancellation schedule with its exact tiers. Compare the total across experts, not the headline rate: a $650-an-hour expert with a $2,000 retainer and a 50 percent cancellation fee can cost less than a $450-an-hour expert with a $10,000 retainer and a 100 percent cancellation fee, depending on how the case moves.

Second, negotiate the cancellation terms, not the hourly rate. Experts rarely move on their rate — it is a market signal, and discounting it signals something they do not want signaled. They will often move on the cancellation window, the retainer application, or the travel policy, because those are logistics, not reputation. And third, calendar the cancellation deadlines alongside the trial deadlines. The most expensive sentence in the engagement letter is the one that says the fee is due in full if notice arrives inside seven days. The cheapest protection against it is a diary entry.
