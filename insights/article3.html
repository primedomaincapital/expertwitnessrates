In 1993, the Supreme Court decided Daubert v. Merrell Dow Pharmaceuticals — a case about whether the anti-nausea drug Bendectin caused birth defects — and rewrote the rules for expert testimony in federal court. The holding was deceptively simple: the trial judge is the gatekeeper. Before an expert reaches a jury, the judge must determine that the testimony rests on reliable principles and methods, reliably applied to the facts. Three decades later, the Daubert motion is the most feared pretrial weapon in complex litigation. An excluded expert is not just a lost opinion. It is lost fees, lost months, and often a lost case.

The standard has only tightened. On December 1, 2023, Federal Rule of Evidence 702 was amended to clarify what many courts had been fudging: the proponent of expert testimony must prove — by a preponderance of the evidence — that the expert's methods are reliable and were reliably applied. The amendment was a signal to judges who had been letting questionable expertise reach juries with a shrug and a "goes to weight, not admissibility." The gate is now explicitly narrower.

Where Experts Actually Get Excluded

The pattern in exclusion rulings is consistent, and it surprises lawyers who assume credentials carry the day. Most exclusions target methodology, not qualifications. The expert is accomplished; the method is not sound. The recurring failure modes are well documented.

First, the unreliable methodology: opinions built on differential diagnoses that do not reliably rule out alternative causes, causation opinions unsupported by the scientific literature, damages models with assumptions that would not survive peer review. PwC's annual analysis of Daubert challenges has consistently shown that financial and damages experts face the highest exclusion rates — their models have the most moving parts, and each assumption is a target.

Second, the unqualified expert: accomplished professionals opining outside their actual domain. The cardiologist testifying about oncology standards. The general contractor offering structural engineering opinions. Judges have grown unsparing here, and the 2023 amendment to Rule 702 sharpened the blade.

Third, the analytical gap — the Supreme Court's phrase from General Electric v. Joiner (1997) for the chasm between the expert's data and their conclusion. The data may be fine. The leap from data to opinion is not. This is where hired-gun testimony usually dies: the conclusion was purchased, and the methodology was reverse-engineered to reach it.

Daubert's Expanding Reach

Daubert began as a rule about scientific testimony. In 1999, Kumho Tire v. Carmichael extended the gatekeeping function to all expert testimony — technical, engineering, financial, and otherwise. The tire-failure expert in Kumho was not a scientist; he was an engineer, and the Court held that trial judges must apply the same reliability screening regardless of discipline. Overnight, every category of expert became challengeable on methodological grounds.

The expansion continued through the rules. The 2000 amendments to Rule 702 codified the Daubert trilogy's requirements — sufficient facts or data, reliable principles and methods, reliable application. The December 2023 amendment went further, placing the burden of proving reliability squarely on the proponent by a preponderance of the evidence and directing courts to keep unreliable expertise from juries rather than waving it through.

The practical consequence: there is no longer any expert category immune from challenge. Damages experts, once considered relatively safe, now face the highest exclusion rates in PwC's annual analyses — their models have the most assumptions, and each assumption is a target. Medical experts face challenges to differential diagnoses that fail to rule out alternatives reliably. Even law-enforcement and forensic experts, long given deference, are facing heightened scrutiny over the scientific foundations of pattern-matching disciplines.

How to Daubert-Proof Your Expert

The defense starts before retention. Vet the expert's prior exclusions — an expert excluded before will be challenged again, with the prior order attached as Exhibit A. Confirm the expert's opinions sit squarely within their active practice, not adjacent to it. Review their publications for contradictions the other side will find in an afternoon.

Then build the record methodically. The expert's report should read like a lab notebook, not a brief: methods stated explicitly, literature cited, alternative explanations addressed and ruled out on stated grounds, assumptions disclosed rather than buried. Every shortcut the expert takes in the report becomes a paragraph in the Daubert motion.

Prepare the expert for the hearing as you would for trial. Judges deciding gatekeeping motions are evaluating the expert's intellectual rigor in real time — evasive answers about methodology read as consciousness of weakness. And consider the preemptive strike: in close cases, some litigators affirmatively seek a Daubert hearing on their own expert to lock in admissibility before trial, rather than defending it mid-stream.

One final reality check. Daubert challenges succeed at meaningful rates, and the trend favors challengers — particularly since the 2023 rule amendment. The lawyers who treat expert selection as a credentials exercise are playing a 1990 game. The modern game is methodological, and it is won or lost in the expert's file long before the motion is filed.

"Under Daubert, the expert's resume gets them into the courtroom. Their methodology decides whether they stay."
