Every litigator has the story. The expert who looked impeccable on paper — Ivy League credentials, a wall of publications — and then melted under cross-examination. Or worse, the expert whose prior testimony, discoverable in about twenty minutes by opposing counsel, contradicted the opinion they were hired to give. The case did not lose on the merits. It lost on the witness.

Finding an expert is easy. There are directories, referral services, and broker firms that will produce a CV within 48 hours. Vetting one is the actual skill, and most lawyers do it badly — skimming credentials, checking the hourly rate, and stopping there. The lawyers who do it well treat vetting as due diligence on a six-figure investment that can decide the case.

Where the Good Ones Actually Come From

The referral network remains the primary market. Partners who have tried cases in the specialty know who survived cross and who did not, and that institutional memory is worth more than any database. Expert broker firms — the Expert Institute, SEAK's directory, Round Table Group, TASA — add speed and breadth, particularly for obscure specialties where your firm's Rolodex runs dry. ExpertPages and similar directories provide the long tail.

Then there is the academic channel, which cuts both ways. University professors bring unimpeachable credentials and genuine independence — juries trust them. But the best testifying experts are often practitioners, not academics: the surgeon still operating, the engineer still stamping drawings, the accountant still auditing. Active practice is armor against the "professional witness" attack, and it keeps the expert's opinions grounded in how the work is actually done.

A note on the channel to avoid leaning on: the expert who finds you. Cold-soliciting experts and expert-marketing firms exist, and some are legitimate. But an expert whose business development consists of chasing lawyers is, definitionally, testifying more than practicing — which is precisely the profile opposing counsel wants to put in front of a jury.

The Vetting Checklist

Credentials are the beginning, not the end. Board certification must be current and in the right specialty for the standard of care at issue — a general surgeon opining on neurosurgical standards is a Daubert motion waiting to happen. License history must be clean; verify state disciplinary actions directly, because experts do not volunteer them.

Prior testimony is where cases are quietly won and lost. Pull the expert's deposition and trial transcripts. Read what they said under oath in other cases, for both plaintiffs and defendants. An expert who has testified exclusively for one side for a decade is a "hired gun" narrative gift-wrapped for opposing counsel. Worse is the expert whose prior opinions contradict the one you need — nothing delights a cross-examiner more than an expert impeached by their own transcript. Check specifically for prior Daubert challenges: an expert who has been excluded before will be challenged again, and the motion practically writes itself.

Publications cut both ways too. A strong publication record establishes authority — but every article is a potential exhibit. If the expert's published views conflict with the opinion they will offer in your case, you will spend the deposition explaining the discrepancy. Review the bibliography before you retain, not after.

Finally, the practical items that get skipped under deadline pressure: deposition demeanor (watch video if it exists — juries punish arrogance), availability through your trial date, conflicts (has this expert consulted for the other side's industry?), and the fee agreement in writing before disclosure deadlines. An expert who will not put rates and terms in writing is an expert who will surprise you later.

The Interview: Questions That Reveal Everything

Before you retain, interview. Thirty minutes on the phone will tell you more than thirty pages of CV. Start with the question most lawyers never ask: "Walk me through a case where your opinion hurt the side that hired you." Every honest expert has one. The expert who claims they have never delivered bad news is either lying or has never been tested — and you do not want to be the first test.

Ask about their last three testimonies, not their last three decades. Recency matters: an expert whose trial experience is all from the 2010s may not know how judges are applying the amended Rule 702. Ask directly whether they have ever been excluded or limited under Daubert, and what happened. The answer matters less than the candor — an expert who discloses a fifteen-year-old limitation and explains what changed is far safer than one who claims a perfect record you later discover is not.

Probe the independence question head-on: "What percentage of your income comes from expert work, and what is your plaintiff-defendant split?" There is no disqualifying answer, but there are answers that require preparation. An expert who earns 90% of their income testifying for plaintiffs needs a compelling story about objectivity before opposing counsel writes one for them.

Finally, test the teaching ability. Ask the expert to explain the core opinion the way they would to a jury. If they cannot make it clear to you — a lawyer who already understands the case — they will not make it clear to twelve strangers. The best experts are teachers first and advocates never. Hire accordingly.

"Retain the expert you'd want if the roles were reversed. Then verify everything they told you."
