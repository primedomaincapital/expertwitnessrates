Expert Witness Rates: Review, Deposition, and Trial Are Three Prices

An expert witness does not have one hourly rate. File review, deposition, and trial testimony are priced separately, and testimony commands a premium over review in nearly every survey. The spread between specialties is wider still: a physician's clinical opportunity cost sets a floor that a non-medical expert never faces. This page uses the SEAK survey — the largest recurring survey of testifying experts — and marks clearly where a figure comes from a secondary summary instead.

Methodology

Included: SEAK's 2024 Survey of Expert Witness Fees (1,633 respondents across more than 250 areas of expertise) as summarized in published coverage, SEAK's own physician-rate guidance, and Expert Institute engagement averages as reported in the same coverage. Specialty figures for surgical subspecialties are labeled as secondary summaries, because they were verified through published rate summaries rather than the SEAK table itself. Excluded: individual experts' advertised rates, which are quotes, not market data; and any prior-year SEAK figure that could not be verified this session — hence Baseline labels on SEAK rows rather than invented trend comparisons.

What the numbers mean

The structure of the pricing is more useful than any single rate. Review work sits at the bottom ($450 median across all experts), deposition above it ($571 average), trial at the top ($633 average). That ladder reflects what is being bought: uninterrupted, cross-examinable time. The specialty spread then does the rest — a surgical subspecialist's trial rate can run triple the all-expert median, because the alternative use of that hour is an operating room. A litigator budgeting from the all-expert median alone will under-budget a surgical case by a wide margin; a litigator budgeting from a neurosurgeon's rate will over-budget everything else.
